Designing T-cell receptors with evolution, not gradients

tcrga is a research project at Friedrich-Alexander-Universität Erlangen-Nürnberg that uses a genetic algorithm to search for T-cell receptor sequences predicted to recognise a peptide you choose. This page explains the problem, why evolution is the right search strategy for it, and — importantly — what the results do and do not mean.

The one-sentence version

Given a short target peptide, tcrga evolves a population of candidate receptor sequences and keeps the ones a predictive model scores as most likely to bind — producing a shortlist of hypotheses, not confirmed binders.

The immune problem, in plain terms

T-cells patrol the body for signs of disease. They do not see whole diseased cells; they see short fragments of protein, called peptides, displayed on a cell's surface by a molecular presenting tray (the MHC complex). Sitting on the T-cell's surface is a T-cell receptor (TCR) — a Y-shaped protein built from two chains, called alpha and beta. If the shape of the receptor's tip fits the presented peptide well enough, the T-cell activates and the cell displaying that peptide is marked for destruction. This is, in outline, how the immune system recognises viral infection and some cancers.

The part of each chain that actually makes contact with the peptide is a short, highly variable loop called the CDR3 (complementarity-determining region 3) — typically 8 to 24 amino acids long. Its exact sequence, on both chains together, determines what the receptor recognises.

Now flip the problem around: given a peptide of interest — say, one associated with a particular infection or tumour — can you find a pair of CDR3 sequences (alpha and beta) predicted to bind it? This is a search over an enormous space. Each CDR3 is a string drawn from a 20-letter amino-acid alphabet at up to 24 positions, and the two chains have to work together: they contribute jointly and non-additively to recognition, so you cannot optimise one chain and then the other. It is a genuine needle-in-a-haystack problem, and the haystack is bigger than can be enumerated.

Why a genetic algorithm

The usual way to search a huge space efficiently is gradient descent: nudge a candidate solution in the direction that a differentiable scoring function says improves it. That requires the scoring function to hand back a gradient — a mathematical derivative — alongside its score.

The scoring functions available for TCR-peptide binding do not do that. They are black boxes: statistical distance measures over known binders, positional weight matrices, gradient-boosted classifiers, or wrapped neural network predictors. Some of them are differentiable internally, but the project deliberately treats every scorer the same way — as a function that takes a candidate sequence pair and peptide and returns a probability, nothing more — so that scorers can be swapped, combined, or replaced without touching the search itself.

A genetic algorithm (GA) is a search strategy modelled loosely on evolution: keep a population of candidate solutions, score each one, preferentially keep and recombine the better-scoring ones, introduce small random changes (mutations), and repeat over many generations. Because it only ever needs a score — never a gradient — it works with any scoring function you can plug in, including ones nobody has made differentiable. That is precisely the situation here, which is why tcrga is built around pymoo, a Python optimisation library, rather than a deep-learning training loop.

What the algorithm is actually optimising

The primary objective is a single number: the predicted probability that a given (alpha, beta) sequence pair binds the target peptide. Two further checks act as guard rails rather than co-equal goals:

A candidate that violates a constraint is treated as infeasible rather than merely penalised, so the search stays pointed squarely at binding while still ruling out sequences that no real thymus would ever produce, or that would likely attack the wrong target.

Where the training data comes from

McPAS-TCR is a manually curated database of TCR sequences linked to known diseases, maintained by the Friedman lab at the Weizmann Institute. It pairs CDR3-alpha and CDR3-beta sequences with the peptide each receptor is known to respond to, which is exactly the kind of labelled example the scoring models need. Anyone using results from this project should cite the database (see the project README for the full citation).

What this is not

Predicted, not proven

Every sequence this tool produces is a predicted binder according to a computational model, not a laboratory-confirmed one. Published TCR-peptide binding predictors are known to generalise poorly to peptides they were not trained on — a well-documented limitation of the whole field, not a shortcoming specific to this project. Treat the output as a prioritised, diverse shortlist of hypotheses for experimental validation, never as a validated result on its own.

Optimising hard against a single predicted score also has a sharp edge: a genetic algorithm will cheerfully find sequences that score very well and mean nothing biologically, if the scoring function has any exploitable weakness. The spec-first design page describes a case of exactly that happening during development, and how it became a permanent safeguard.
